# Write Lecture Notes: figures

Apply these standards within the scope selected in [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md). This reference does not initiate a separate review pass or expand the task.

## Concept-driven figures and visual explanations

### Plan visual coverage before drafting

- Audit every worked example for its important relationships: those central to its teaching purpose, such as the main mechanism, comparison, uncertainty, or failure mode. Identify what changes and what is held fixed. Visualize these important relationships where a display makes them clearer; do not aim to visualize every relationship or incidental calculation; explicitly reuse a nearby figure when appropriate instead of duplicating it. A diagram of a computational workflow alone does not show the statistical relationship. Maintain high-quality example plots: make the intended contrast perceptible, preserve comparable axes and consistent encodings, label units and uncertainty correctly, and state a clear take-home message in the accompanying interpretation or caption. Check coverage against the example's central teaching claims, verify numerical values, and inspect figures at their final reading size. Do not force plots for relationships that have no useful visual representation.

- Choose each panel for an inferential purpose, not visual symmetry or a default EDA recipe. A marginal histogram belongs only when predictor coverage, sampling imbalance, or another relevant distributional feature is part of the argument. To motivate nonlinear regression, contrasting response--covariate scatterplots may be more useful; to explain smoothing, overlay the fitted estimate and compare tuning choices or show a purposeful diagnostic. Keep data and axes comparable when isolating a tuning effect, and distinguish visible roughness from sampling bias or variance.

- Treat figures as part of the mathematical and pedagogical development, alongside exposition, worked examples, and exercises. For a substantive module revision, assess visual needs across the entire requested module before spending the budget on prose edits. Assess whether existing figures actually cover the important teaching relationships; retain them when they do, and add or revise figures only for a substantive explanatory need.
- Make a compact authoring plan mapping each major concept to its learning difficulty, the visual insight students should gain, an existing or proposed figure, and its priority. Keep this planning material outside the student notes. Inspect what existing figures actually explain; a figure about one concept does not cover other concepts merely because it appears in the same subsection.
- Give highest priority to concepts central to the learning objectives whose mechanism, geometry, variation, or failure mode is difficult to infer from formulas alone. Strong candidates include abstract definitions with concrete geometry; distinctions students commonly confuse; assumptions whose violation changes a conclusion; limiting behavior across sample sizes; competing sources of error; parameter or tuning sensitivity; and relationships between individual and simultaneous behavior. Importance and explanatory value determine priority, not ease of plotting or order of appearance.
- **Develop a substantive figure for each high-priority visual need.** One coherent figure can cover several related needs when those connections are explicit. A subsection with several independent central ideas may need several figures. Do not impose a fixed figure-per-page or figure-per-definition quota, and do not add weak figures to satisfy a count. Every substantive subsection must nevertheless receive an explicit visual-coverage assessment; a text-only subsection needs a pedagogical reason, not merely an inherited absence of figures.
- Reuse a figure when it fulfills the identified teaching purpose; revise or replace it when it does not. A photograph, decorative illustration, table, or schematic that merely restates labels does not substitute for explaining an important mechanism. When the user requests more visualization, prioritize missing conceptual coverage and deeper visual explanations in addition to improving existing graphics.

### Develop the visual argument

- Audit visual maturity against the intended audience. For graduate notes, omit plots that merely illustrate elementary arithmetic, a point lying off a line, an obvious monotone transformation, or equations redrawn as boxes. Simplicity is welcome when it exposes a consequential mechanism; complexity is not a proxy for rigor. Require each figure or panel to add explanatory value beyond the nearby calculation, and remove redundant displays and verbose justifications. A mathematically faithful schematic can be preferable to a numerical plot when it directly reveals geometry, conditional dependence, information flow, or a limiting relationship. Choose the representation for the insight, not a quota of plots or numerical detail.

- If a sound teaching purpose produces a weak visual contrast, reconsider the experiment before changing colors or adding annotations. Choose sample size, parameter regimes, controls, or paired comparisons that expose the important relationship while preserving the stated assumptions. Distinguish a substantive contrast between regimes from agreement checks within each regime; do not exaggerate effects through misleading axes or selective realizations. Explain the design choice and leave the central exercise investigation for students.
- Prefer probability density plots when the teaching goal is to compare distributional shape, location, spread, or concentration; mark relevant means, thresholds, or tail areas. Use CDFs when cumulative probabilities, stochastic ordering, quantiles, or empirical-distribution checks are the actual target. Choose the display for intuitive access to the intended relationship.
- Preserve the purpose of an experiment when changing its display. If the lesson includes agreement between repeated simulation and theory, show the simulated results as well as the theoretical law; analytic densities alone remove that lesson. For density comparisons, use density-scaled histograms with exact densities overlaid, common bin boundaries and axis limits across comparable panels, and a stated repetition count and seed. Show ordinary finite-simulation variation without implying that agreement proves the result. Choose repetitions for the teaching purpose rather than treating one example's count as universal.
- A figure may teach two complementary lessons: simulation versus theory within each panel, and the effect of a changed condition across panels. Identify both explicitly and design the experiment to separate them. Use paired datasets or shared randomness when that isolates the comparison; do not add panels that repeat the same lesson.
- Before drawing, state the question the figure answers and the conclusion the reader should be able to explain from it. Specify the model or construction, the quantities shown, what varies, what remains fixed, and the comparison that makes the idea visible. Choose the representation to suit the reasoning: a plot, geometric diagram, sampling display, distributional comparison, or workflow schematic.
- Build an explanatory sequence when the concept needs one. For example, connect data to an estimator and then its error; compare a condition that holds with a counterexample; or show an unscaled quantity beside its centered and rescaled version. Prefer two or more coordinated panels when each adds a distinct inferential step. Use common scales for direct comparisons, identify deliberate scale changes, and distinguish truth, observations, estimates, and uncertainty consistently. Do not force paired panels when a single developed diagram explains the idea fully, and do not squeeze panels into the space of one plot at the expense of readability.
- Make the mechanism visible through purposeful annotations: mark a bias or approximation gap, tangent slope, risk-set change, coverage failure, neighborhood, threshold, or limiting object where it matters. Plotting several curves without showing what their differences mean is not a developed explanation. Use enough settings or stages to reveal the behavior, but avoid unrelated panels and crowded parameter sweeps.
- Useful statistical designs include a sampling distribution centered away from the target to distinguish bias from variance; pointwise intervals beside repeated simultaneous-coverage outcomes; original and rescaled sampling laws across sample sizes; a transformation with its tangent and the resulting uncertainty; or a bandwidth sequence paired with its bias--variance consequences. Select designs relevant to the actual material rather than inserting this list into every module. Analogous geometric, causal, temporal, and comparative explanations apply in other disciplines.
- For conceptual workflows and algorithms, show meaningful dependencies, branches, repeated draws, and return arrows for revision or iteration. A one-way chain is inadequate when the reasoning is cyclic. Use a workflow schematic when it clarifies a nontrivial dependency or procedure; do not turn obvious steps into boxes merely to provide another figure.
- Integrate each figure into the exposition near the definition, result, or worked example it supports, preserving definition–example and result–proof–example sequences. Introduce what to look for, refer to specific panels or annotated features, and interpret the finding afterward. The caption must state the setup, explain the encodings or panels, and give the principal lesson and any essential limitation. A student should understand why the figure is there without opening a script or companion. Where useful, have a nearby exercise ask students to predict, explain, or extend the displayed behavior.

### Mathematical fidelity and reproducibility

- Draw numerical curves from the stated equations or fitted procedures. Define the data, parameters, domain, tuning choices, and numerical approximation well enough to reproduce the display. Keep generating code or native vector source with the course assets; print the essential setup and interpretation in the notes.
- Distinguish schematic geometry, exact calculations, numerical approximations, and simulated results. Do not draw an arbitrary curve and label it as an actual optimizer, sampling law, confidence band, or fitted estimate. A schematic is appropriate for a relationship, but its geometry must respect that relationship and its caption must say it is schematic.
- Match the evidence to the claim. One realization can illustrate an estimator but cannot establish bias, variance, expected risk, or coverage. Use exact distributions or repeated sampling for these quantities; state sample sizes, replication counts, random seeds, and relevant Monte Carlo uncertainty. Do not imply a uniform guarantee or general ranking from a finite grid or selected examples. Include a contrasting regime or failure case when it is central to understanding the result's scope.

### Production priorities and visual verification

- Choose efficient implementations after deciding what deserves explanation. Prefer analytic curves, native vector diagrams, modest numerical calculations, or reproducible simulations with minimal dependencies. Computational cost should usually change the implementation, not eliminate a high-priority figure: simplify the model, reduce the grid, or use an exact small example while retaining the intended insight. Reserve time for essential figures before optional exposition expansion, peripheral examples, or cosmetic refinements.
- If an essential figure cannot be completed accurately within a real constraint, identify the uncovered concept and report that visual requirement as incomplete. Do not silently replace it with prose and claim full completion. Conversely, stop refining a figure once its mathematics, explanatory content, and readability are sound; elaborate rendering and repeated cosmetic changes are not the objective.
- Design for the final reading size. Use plot text comparable to nearby caption text, informative axis labels and units, accessible contrast, and line styles or symbols that remain distinguishable without color. Check legends, tick labels, annotations, and panel labels at the actual size in the compiled notes. Increase space or split the figure when necessary; do not shrink important labels to fit.
- Inspect the rendered figure pages, not only source code or thumbnails. Check mathematical fidelity, misleading axis ranges, panel comparability, label collisions, clipping, caption accuracy, and proximity to the discussion. Recheck a figure after any change to its content, size, or layout. A successful compile is not visual verification.

### Exploratory data analysis when introducing a dataset

- When introducing a dataset in the notes, include purposeful exploratory plots made with **ggplot2**, placed with its introduction to motivate modeling. Use only the views that advance that purpose; one informative view may suffice. Do not add a histogram or extra panel merely to complete a layout. Reuse this introduction when the same dataset recurs, adding a display only for a new question. For an exercise-only dataset, provide contextual exploration without revealing the central plot or comparison students are asked to produce.
- Establish what each row represents, variables and units, sample size, grouping or time structure, missing values, and any filtering or transformations. Use the same analyzed observations in the plots and subsequent calculations. Appropriate views include a response--predictor scatterplot, marginal histogram or empirical CDF, group comparisons, or an ordered time plot. For small samples, show individual points; for repeated values, preserve multiplicity with transparency, counts, or clearly disclosed jitter. If only summaries are supplied, plot the available group summaries or sample sizes and state that raw-data features cannot be recovered; never invent observations to make an EDA plot.
- Introduce the question each plot addresses and interpret the observed pattern immediately afterward: scale and range, predictor coverage, association, possible nonlinearity, uneven spread, outliers, group differences, or missingness. Use the exploration to motivate model assumptions and later checks. Distinguish a marginal response distribution from conditional regression errors, descriptive association from causation, and data-suggested hypotheses from independent validation. A preliminary fitted regression line and complementary residual diagnostics may accompany the introduction when they help assess the proposed model; explain their preliminary role and define the quantities at the appropriate point. Keep full posterior predictive analysis with its developed methodology. Combine a marginal histogram with a scatterplot when aligned axes improve the comparison, and remove redundant panels.
- Keep reproducible R/ggplot2 source with the course assets and add it to the existing computational companion when that companion is within scope. Specify bins, transformations, units, and any smoother; seed stochastic plotting operations when used. Use readable themes, accessible colors, clear legends, and vector output where suitable. Show the actual plots and interpretations in the notes, not only code or a link. Verify the plotted data and inspect the rendered panels at their final reading size.
