# Write Lecture Notes: exposition

Apply these standards within the scope selected in [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md). This reference does not initiate a separate review pass or expand the task.

## Organize the exposition

- Make the section prelude brief but intellectually substantive: explain what the subject studies, why that question matters, and why the approach developed here is needed rather than a simpler familiar model. Use a concrete limitation or phenomenon to create the need; avoid merely announcing topics or saying a method is flexible. Do not include learning objectives, skills-to-acquire lists, computational conventions, or technical housekeeping. This section-level rule does not remove the required subsection boxes below.
- Move technical remarks from section preludes into the corresponding subsection or subsubsection, after the concepts and prerequisites they need and beside the result, method, or example they qualify. Preserve useful content and repair numbering and cross-references; do not retain an early technical remark merely to announce a convention before its context exists.
- Treat notation and computational conventions as requirements for the entire exposition, including formulas, proofs, examples, algorithms, exercises, figures, and any requested companion. Enforce them in the actual material rather than announcing compliance in a prelude. Introduce essential local notation or implementation details at their first relevant use; a convention statement elsewhere does not excuse inconsistent usage.

- Start each subsection on a new page, except the first subsection within a section, which may follow the section introduction on the same page. In LaTeX, insert `\newpage` immediately before each subsequent `\subsection` heading; do not apply this rule to subsubsections.
- **Every subsection must contain exactly one of each visibly labeled box: Core Reading, Guiding Questions, Review Questions, and Further Reading. This single set of four boxes is mandatory, not optional or conditional on usefulness.** Use the course style's existing box environments; ordinary prose, an unboxed heading, exercises, or boxes elsewhere in the section do not substitute for a subsection's own boxes. If a needed environment is missing, add a reusable environment consistent with the course style rather than silently omitting the box.
- The subsection is the unit for these four boxes. Its subsubsections are supporting divisions within the main exposition: they do not require their own Core Reading, Guiding Questions, Review Questions, or Further Reading boxes, and must not repeat the subsection-level set. Consolidate relevant material from all supporting subsubsections into the parent subsection's unique boxes, with content tailored to that subsection. In LaTeX, apply this rule to `\subsection`, not `\subsubsection`.
- Use this order: subsection heading and invisible labels/setup; Core Reading box; brief motivating prelude; Guiding Questions box; main exposition with worked examples and exercises; Review Questions box; Further Reading box. Core Reading and Guiding Questions belong before the main exposition; Review Questions and Further Reading close the subsection. Do not bury the opening boxes after examples, figures, or derivations.
- Give chapters and subsections concise academic titles. Audit headings and titled environments for conversational, imperative, or task-oriented wording and replace these with precise subject names. Write actual questions in the required Guiding Questions box; use concise academic titles for any additional thematic subtitles.
- Make Guiding Questions short, engaging statistical or inferential questions that create a reason to study the material. Prefer a concrete tension, surprising possibility, or consequential comparison. Avoid packed lists of technical topics, procedural checklists, and questions that simply restate the subsection title. Keep each question simple and connect its eventual answer to the exposition.
- Introduce the motivation and inferential or conceptual target before formal machinery. Tailor the content of every required box to the subsection rather than filling it with generic boilerplate. Choose definitions, results, examples, and investigations to support that development; this flexibility does not permit omitting the four required boxes.
- Reserve optional concept, takeaway, and important-summary boxes for insights that are both important and concise: a central inferential lesson, consequential distinction, or useful synthesis that deserves emphasis and can be stated in a few short sentences or a compact formula with interpretation. Do not box routine exposition, a topic preview, or a loose collection of facts. Also place key results that students should memorize or retain for later use in a concise summary box. This includes a compact set of foundational commitments or organizing principles that students should remember, not just formulas and theorems. When a short bullet list answers a central question such as why a framework matters or what governs its practice, consider a titled summary box if the points form a substantive, memorable synthesis. Do not dismiss such a synthesis as a topic preview merely because it appears early; tighten the wording and move extended explanations outside the box. Select only essential formulas, identities, or principles; include the assumptions or conditions needed to use them correctly and a brief interpretation where helpful. Purposeful repetition of a key result is appropriate as a memory aid, but do not duplicate its proof or extended explanation. If other material needs several explanatory paragraphs or merely repeats nearby text without serving this retention purpose, integrate it into ordinary prose instead. Audit existing optional boxes for this standard and remove the box when the content does not justify emphasis. This selectivity does not remove the four required subsection boxes or change the course style for formal definitions and results.
- At the opening of a subsection or subsubsection that begins with an example, add a concise motivation before the example. Focus the introduction on the concept and why it matters, rather than previewing the sequence of topics to follow.
- Explain what assumptions mean, why a calculation matters, and how a conclusion depends on the model. Distinguish formal claims from interpretation, caveats, and broader discussion.
- Build from a simple instance toward the general result and, when useful, return to the instance to interpret it. Use schematics when they clarify a procedure, dependency, hierarchy, or relationship; give them informative captions and explain them in the text.
- Populate both required reading boxes with specific, verified sources and relevant chapters or sections. Core Reading prepares the reader for the subsection; Further Reading offers a distinct extension, deeper treatment, or alternative perspective and briefly explains its relevance. Reading the further material may be optional for students, but including the Further Reading box is mandatory for the author. Verify references rather than inventing them or treating an unverified reference as a reason to omit the box. If verification is genuinely unavailable, retain the labeled box, state the unresolved reference explicitly, and report that requirement as incomplete rather than claiming the subsection is finished.
- End each subsection's main material with a required Review Questions box that tests conceptual connections, interpretation, assumptions, and limitations developed there. Revisit the opening Guiding Questions where appropriate. Review Questions complement the substantive numbered exercises; neither replaces the other.
## Definitions and mathematical results

- Audit inline definitions throughout exposition, captions, examples, and exercises. When prose introduces a reusable concept (for example “is called,” “is defined as,” or a newly emphasized technical term), merge its defining statement into a closely related definition or create a standalone numbered definition. State its objects, domain, assumptions, and defining condition there, then follow it immediately with a worked example. Keep equivalences requiring justification and interpretive cautions in adjacent exposition or results. A caption or exercise may recall the definition or specify local notation, but should not be the only home of a concept used elsewhere. Do not promote every temporary symbol, theorem-specific quantity, or already-defined term into a new definition. Check prerequisites, numbering, and cross-references after consolidation.
- Write each definition as a formal, self-contained statement: specify the objects, domains, assumptions, and defining condition. Keep interpretation, warnings, and qualifications that are not part of the definition in adjacent prose.
- Follow **every definition immediately** with a substantive worked example that instantiates its terms and shows how to use or recognize the concept. Place interpretation and cautions after that example when possible. If an existing sequence prevents this, reorganize it.
- Follow **every theorem, proposition, and lemma** with a proof or proof sketch. Prefer a focused proof sketch when a full proof is long or peripheral. Give the full argument when the proof technique itself is an instructional objective or a gap would obscure the result. State any omitted steps or external dependencies honestly.
- After the proof, give a focused calculation or worked example that exposes a consequence, verifies a non-obvious mechanism, or identifies a limitation of the result. Do not append arbitrary numerical substitution merely to satisfy the sequence. Multiple examples are welcome when each adds a distinct pedagogical point; do not impose a fixed quota.
- Audit each example for a distinct teaching claim, stated clearly at the beginning and demonstrated by its calculation, comparison, or figure. At beginning graduate level, favor mechanisms, contrasting regimes, counterexamples, identifiability, statistical versus computational limitations, and consequences for inference or practice. A motivational sentence cannot rescue a trivial body. Remove, merge, or deepen examples that merely repeat nearby prose, substitute arbitrary numbers, or teach the same lesson again. Close with the consequence supported by the evidence, not generic praise for the method.
- Preserve simple concrete examples that instantiate an abstract definition: small matrices, explicit resamples, and elementary cases can make an unfamiliar object usable. Simplicity is not the defect; lack of a new conceptual purpose is. Keep the mandatory definition--example sequence without inflating each illustration into an investigation. For other examples, a familiar definition followed by elementary arithmetic is usually insufficient at graduate level.
- Match every example plot to its teaching claim: state what varies, what is held fixed, and which visible feature supports the conclusion. Revise or remove panels that contribute no additional insight; reuse suitable figures instead of adding one per example. Keep motivation concise and avoid generic introductions such as “this illustrates the result.”
- State the scope and assumptions of a result precisely. Check algebra, dimensions, domains, special cases, and any numerical values. Do not use an illustrative calculation as a substitute for proof.
