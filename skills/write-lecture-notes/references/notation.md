## Statistical notation conventions

- Use concise boldface notation for complete data vectors: $\mathbf y=(y_1,\ldots,y_n)^\top$ and $\mathbf Y$ for its random counterpart. State this convention briefly at its first use and specify dimensions or sample size in the setup; avoid redundant sample-size superscripts such as $\mathbf y^n$. Apply it consistently to fitted and replicated vectors while preserving meaningful draw or iteration indices, such as $\mathbf Y_{\mathrm{rep}}^{(s)}$. Do not remove actual powers or sample-size notation for unbolded sequences by mechanical replacement. This explicit introductory convention is an exception to the default of keeping notation-policy prose outside the notes.

- Set vectors and matrices in boldface throughout formulas, prose, algorithms, figures, and exercises. Preserve the letter convention for their mathematical role: for example, $\mathbf{X}$ for a random vector, $\mathbf{x}$ for its realization, and $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ for a vector parameter. Exception: use ordinary $\Sigma$ for a matrix parameter; it need not be boldface. Use the course style's existing bold-math commands; use `\mathbf` for Roman symbols and `\boldsymbol` (or an existing equivalent) for Greek symbols. Keep scalars and individual scalar entries unbolded. Apply this convention implicitly, without explanatory notation-policy prose in the notes.
- Use uppercase Roman letters for random variables and lowercase Roman letters for their realizations, for example $X_i$ and $x_i$. Here Roman means the Latin alphabet, not a requirement for upright mathematical type. Preserve established subject-specific exceptions: use $\varepsilon_i$ for additive regression or observation errors and $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ for their vector or matrix, rather than mechanically renaming them $E_i$ or $\mathbf E$. Distinguish unobserved model errors from fitted residuals such as $e_i=y_i-\widehat y_i$. Retain meaningful established innovation symbols in state-space models. Audit the same error symbols through model statements, proofs, examples, exercises, algorithms, and companion explanations; do not replace expectation operators, exposure variables, or unrelated uses of the same letter.
- Use Greek letters for model parameters, including parameter blocks, binary parameters, and their prior/posterior draws: for example $\theta$, $\phi$, $\mu$, and $\sigma^2$. Bayesian parameters remain Greek even though they are uncertain. Do not rename parameters as Roman random variables such as $A,B$ merely to state a posterior sampling theorem; write $p(\theta,\phi\mid x^n)$ and draws $(\theta^{(s)},\phi^{(s)})$. Reserve uppercase/lowercase Roman pairs such as $X_i,x_i$ for sampling variables and their realizations.
- Use lowercase Roman letters with subscript $0$ for prior hyperparameters, for example $m_0$, $a_0$, and $b_0$. The index $0$ denotes the state before observing data.
- Use the corresponding lowercase Roman letters with subscript $n$ for conjugately updated hyperparameters, for example $m_n$, $a_n$, and $b_n$, where $n$ is the sample size. Preserve the same base letter for the same quantity before and after updating, and state the sample size and parameterization explicitly.
- Apply notation conventions implicitly: use the correct symbols without inserting prose about following the convention, letter case, or symbol-renaming policy. Define mathematical objects and parameterizations where needed, but keep editorial notation instructions in this skill. If earlier notation differs, give only a concise mathematical bridge when needed for understanding; avoid a notation tutorial or unnecessary alias table.
- Audit notation by mathematical role in definitions, theorem statements and proofs, worked examples, exercises, algorithms, and figures. A paragraph explaining the convention does not compensate for inconsistent formulas. Apply these conventions consistently in companion material within the requested scope. A request to update this skill alone does not require renaming symbols throughout existing notes.
